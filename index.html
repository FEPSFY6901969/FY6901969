Clean Water Unveiled: The Science of Water Purification

Abstract:

In this article I will be speaking about the importance of drinking water quality control and how it is done. Drinking water needs to be controlled because if not pathogens like bacteria, viruses, and protozoa can cause waterborne diseases such as cholera and campylobacteriosis. Heavy metals (for example - lead, arsenic and mercury), nitrates, and pollutants can cause serious health problems such as chronic health risks, including cancer and neurological disorders. Poor quality control of drinking water can lead to contaminated wastewater, which in turn impacts the ecosystems. This could then lead to pathogen outbreaks and industrial spills. In this article we will also look over a real-life example of where drinking water was not controlled properly.

How is water purified?

Water purification is a very complex and multifunctional process in which water is made safe to drink and for other uses by humans. Large-sized debris, such as leaves, sticks, and trash, are removed through the process of screening, where coarse screens or grates catch these materials to prevent them from passing on to further treatment processes. The next step is coagulation and flocculation, which deals with small suspended particles, organic matter, and dissolved substances. In coagulation, chemicals such as alum or ferric chloride is added to destabilize the colloidal particles. These positively charged coagulants neutralize the negative charges on impurities, which in turn allows them to clump into larger clusters. Next is flocculation, in which gentle mixing allows for the formation of larger flocs, or aggregates of particles, sometimes with the addition of polymers to increase their strength and cohesion. The next step, sedimentation, involves separating these flocs from the water. In a sedimentation tank, gravity pulls the flocs to the bottom, while clearer water is skimmed from the top. This process dramatically reduces the amount of suspended particles in the water, making it ready for finer filtration. Filtration involves the passage of water through multi-layered filters containing sand, gravel, and sometimes anthracite coal to remove the remaining suspended solids and microorganisms. The process continues with the more sophisticated filtration techniques of microfiltration and ultrafiltration, which remove bacteria and protozoa, while RO systems remove dissolved salts, heavy metals, and other microscopic impurities, thereby allowing as high as 99% removal of impurities. Once the water has been filtered, disinfection is applied to neutralize or destroy disease-causing pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, and protozoa. It is usually done by chlorination, ozonation, or UV disinfection. Chlorination, with chlorine gas or hypochlorite solutions, does not only inactivate pathogens but also leaves behind a residual disinfectant that prevents recontamination during distribution. Ozonation involves the application of ozone gas, an effective oxidizing method for disinfection without leaving residuals. On the other hand, UV disinfection uses high-intensity ultraviolet light that destroys the DNA of microorganisms, making them inactive. Following disinfection, the water is stabilized to make its chemical characteristics optimal and compatible with the distribution systems. This may be done by the addition of lime, soda ash, or CO₂ to prevent pipe corrosion. Calcium and magnesium, two essential minerals, may also be added again, especially in water treated either by reverse osmosis or desalination process, which often strip beneficial minerals. Fluoride can be added into the water for teeth decay prevention in some areas. Where necessary, more advanced forms of treatment are employed, addressing more specific pollutants and emerging contaminants: such as advanced oxidation processes- involving oxidants such as ozone or hydrogen peroxide combined with UV to decompose organic pollutants and pharmaceuticals. Adsorption techniques, most often with activated carbon, remove unpleasant tastes, odors, pesticides, herbicides, and by-products of chlorine disinfection. Ion exchange methods can remove nitrates, sulfates, and minerals causing hardness, such as calcium and magnesium. Hard water and soft water are mainly different in mineral content, more precisely in the concentration of calcium (Ca²⁺) and magnesium (Mg²⁺) ions. These differences define their properties, behavior in everyday applications, and treatment needs. Hard water positive effects: provide calcium for bone and teeth growth; provide magnesium for muscle and nerve connections. Minerals in hard water aid in hydration and cellular activity. Harmful effects of hard water - Ingestion in excess, especially of the very hard water, can, in people prone to it, lead to the formation of kidney stones. Deposits (lime scale) in kettles, pipes, and boilers. • Hard water can leave mineral buildups that can make your skin dry, irritated, and your hair dull or brittle. Too high sulfates in hard water lead to temporary diarrhea. Advantages of soft water Soft water washes off entirely and doesn't leave residues that can cause dryness and irritation. Easier to drink because of a much more agreeable flavor - there isn't this acrid taste as produced by the metallic flavor prominent in some hard water. Negative effects of soft water - Softened water replaces the calcium and magnesium, especially during the ion exchange, with sodium. Intake of too much sodium might be a risk, mainly among people suffering from hypertension and heart conditions. Salts are also removed through distillation by evaporating and re-condensing the water, and reverse osmosis systems boast an even finer degree of contaminant removal. Waste management is an aspect of water treatment that ensures responsibility with respect to by-products from water purification. Sludge consisting of captured particles and coagulants from sedimentation and filtration treatment steps needs to be treated further for safe disposal. Similarly, concentrated brine produced by reverse osmosis and desalination treatment requires prudent management to minimize environmental harm caused by salinity imbalances in receiving ecosystems. Each step in this process, from initial screening through to the advanced treatment and waste stages, is critical to making polluted water into a safe, high-quality resource.
